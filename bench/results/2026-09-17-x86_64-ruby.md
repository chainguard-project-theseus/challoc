  30): the 8 KiB buffers of `sample`/`sort` land in mimalloc's larger
  segments.
- gc_churn, the row shaped like Ruby's GC (long-lived medium buffers, bursts
  of short-lived ones freed by sweep): peak RSS 384 MiB against jemalloc's
  368 (+4.2%), mimalloc's 355 and glibc's 317 (+21%: glibc calls
  `malloc_trim` after every full GC in the `--without-jemalloc` build and
  consolidates the brk top). After the 10 forced full GCs all four
  allocators give back about 2% (Ruby's own heap pages): jemalloc's
  `dirty_decay_ms` and challoc's are both 10 s, the process lives 2 s, and
  the `--with-jemalloc` Ruby never calls `malloc_trim`. (On the aarch64 VM
  Wolfi's 64 KiB-page jemalloc build gives back 48% here; see that file.)
- Where challoc stands on Ruby's allocation shape: Ruby 3.4 keeps objects up
  to 640 bytes in its own mmap'ed slots, so malloc sees medium buffers
  (strings over 640 bytes, arrays over 78 elements, st_tables, iseq and
  method structures) with the GC freeing them in bursts. On the interpreter
  rows that shape costs challoc nothing against jemalloc (time within noise,
  lower max RSS, 15% ahead on the realloc-heavy row); the fixed costs show
  as 1 ms on a 42 ms process; the burst-free row shows challoc holding 4%
  more of the burst than jemalloc at peak. All rows are within the task's
  10% band on time and max RSS; no regression to fix.
