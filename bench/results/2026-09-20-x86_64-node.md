## Rows more than 10% behind the best of glibc and jemalloc (plan Phase 3 task 5 acceptance)

- None. `startup` is +4.3% on time and +3.7% on RSS against glibc (0.024 vs 0.023 s median, at bench/rt's 1 ms resolution; the sorted lists 0.023..0.027 and 0.020..0.026 overlap) and equal to jemalloc; the aarch64 file attributes the same startup mechanism (about 270 more `mprotect` calls and 880 more minor faults from committing the first slab of each size class over the `PROT_NONE` class reservations, system time only). `json_churn` is 7.1% faster than glibc and 1.1% faster than jemalloc. The server row is 5.5% more req/s than glibc with a p99 31% lower, within 1.5% of jemalloc's req/s with a 10..18% lower p99; RSS peak is +3.5% against jemalloc and +0.7% against glibc, VmHWM +3.0% / +0.9%, and only the final sample is close to the threshold (+9.8% against glibc, +7.8% against jemalloc: 138.8 MiB vs 126.3 and 128.8; the samples show glibc and jemalloc trim between bursts while challoc's stays flat at 138.8 MiB). The end-of-load mapping table places it: 62.0 MiB `challoc:huge`, the freed 1 MiB `Buffer.allocUnsafe` backing stores retained for reuse up to `huge_retain_max` (64 MiB by default, `src/huge.rs` `DEFAULT_RETAIN_MAX`; 37.0 MiB on aarch64 in the same row, where the request rate was 30% lower), 6.7 MiB `challoc:classes`, 1.9 MiB `challoc:meta`. It is under the 10% threshold; if the huge retention is to be revisited for Node-shaped consumers (a lower `huge_retain_max` or decay of retained huge blocks), that is lane B's `src/purge.rs`/`src/policy.rs`.
